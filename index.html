The app ↗
02 · Concept development

Viewers have too much content and too little time, so deciding what to watch has become a task of its own.

I chose an app format because it is the most accessible way to reach people where they already choose what to watch. I explored how people use streaming services today through user research.

The first version was a high-fidelity prototype. I later rebuilt it as a working, installable app.

03 · Research · Context

Why it matters

Information overload, services split across many platforms, rising subscription costs, infinite-scroll design and “media fatigue” all make choosing harder.

Every day there are hours of new content uploaded, and it would take years to view everything being produced. 50% of TV viewers feel there are too many streaming services, and 59% of consumers say they’re willing to cancel a subscription after watching what they wanted. Users don’t have any control over what is available to watch, and the cost keeps rising: Netflix’s $8 monthly subscription used to be enough, but the premium tier now costs $26.99 a month.

44%of streamers say they often have a hard time finding something to watch (Karrer)
59%of consumers are willing to cancel a subscription after watching their desired content (Karrer)
$26.99a month for Netflix’s premium tier in 2026, up from $8 (9to5Mac)

07 · Build

From wireframes to a working app

I took mood search from wireframes to an installable phone app. You describe how you feel, and Trace recommends real films and shows you can watch on your services.

15+published versions, each reviewed on my phone and refined, usually within a day
53moods, from “cozy” to “midnight urban pursuit”, drawn from words people used in the survey

  2. Real catalogTMDB data for every movie and show, with posters, ratings, runtimes and US streaming availability.

08 · Features

Key features & why

Every feature answers a problem from the research: too many options, and recommendations that don’t explain themselves.

Mood search

Type a feeling in your own words. Trace shows what it understood as removable chips, then 8 picks with match scores, because people think in moods, not genres.

Strict service filter

Only titles on the services you pick, with brand-colored tags. Recommending something you can’t watch was the top frustration.

Live suggestions

Typing “tear” suggests “tear-jerker”, helping people find the right words and catching title searches early.

Pick for me

One top pick, three more and a “Try something different” pick, each with a reason. No typing, and no taste bubble.

Honest matching

Scores are capped so “Strong match” stays meaningful, and anything below 70% is labeled a best guess.

My List

Want to watch and Watched lists, filtered by films or shows, because saving is the natural end of a good search.

Two decisions changed through use: a full-screen rating pop-up became a small bar that fades on its own, and accounts were built, then switched off, because signing in before seeing any value worked against the promise of fewer steps.

09 · Working with AI

I set the direction and made the design calls. Claude wrote and tested the code.

I owned the goals, features, interaction and visual decisions, the mood vocabulary and what to cut. I briefed Claude the way I’d brief a developer, in plain language with examples, and made the final call on every result.

Every release was checked by automated phone-size browser tests, screenshots and my own use: scripted journeys, regression runs, mood accuracy searches and accessibility basics like clear labels and announced changes.

Outcome

A working, installable app that turns a feeling into a short list you can watch tonight.

It costs almost nothing to run, because mood matching happens on the device and the data sources are free.

KinetikIQ

A motion-capture analysis app that gives athletes, patients and the teams around them instant analytics to capture movement, quantify performance and track change over time.

As the sole designer, I worked with the commercial and development teams to design the product UI and UX, commercial materials and social content, building on the existing KinetikIQ brand.

01 · Overview

As the only designer, I shaped KinetikIQ’s design from end to end, from product UI and UX to commercial and social materials, working closely with the commercial and development teams.

The problem

No structure, no polish

There was no structure or consistency. The product didn’t look modern or polished, and it wasn’t inviting.

The approach

One direction, less clinical

I tied together the app’s core need and the visual direction it required, then began polishing its look. The goal was a cleaner, less medical feel across every design.

The result
“I have been using KinetikIQ for several months now, and what I appreciate most is the ability to measure and objectively assess movement. The markerless analysis is fast, simple, and the results appear immediately on the iPad. Whether in the clinic or out on the field, I can assess the quality of movement of my patients and athletes within seconds. With athletes undergoing post-ACL rehabilitation, or during strength and conditioning work, the tool has helped me better support my decisions and make the follow-up process much more tangible for them.” Florian Escarpit · Sports Physiotherapist, ESCA Physio (France)
02 · Key product work

Reports & Home

Four redesigns that changed how clients use the product.

Reports

Clients needed results they could act on. The old report was a dense table of angles and velocities. I designed new Quick Session and Longitudinal reports for both athletes and groups, so performance coaches and clinicians can see exactly what changed, track progress over time and explain it to their athletes and patients. The redesign is currently in development.

Home

The old home screen felt flat and made it hard to move around the app. I kept the interactive body map but made it more inviting, refreshed the cards, and added a bottom navigation bar so every section of the app is one tap away.

02 · Key product work

Exercise selection & Capture

Four redesigns that changed how clients use the product.

Exercise selection

Users had to keep going back to the start to switch between modules. I added module tabs directly on the exercise list, so they can move between Mobility, Dynamic, Postural and Jumps without leaving the page.

Capture screen

I modernized the capture screen and gave the athlete and the exercise their own lines, so it’s clear who is being recorded and what they’re doing. I also added a ‘View protocol’ button, which makes it obvious that the protocol instructions can be opened right from the recording view.

03 · Concept

Where I’d take it next

A simpler flow built around one question at a time: who you’re assessing and which exercise, with the camera always one tap away and dedicated Athletes, Playlists and Assessments tabs.

Accessify

UX research

A one-stop shop for people with disabilities looking to explore their immediate surroundings.

What I made
Competitive analysis, information architecture, user flow, wireframes
01 · Problem

Finding out too late

Many people with disabilities aren’t given proper accommodations when they travel or visit services. People go to parks, concerts and more, only to realize after buying a ticket or spending a lot of time that the place doesn’t have the accessibility services they need.

01 · Solution

Accessibility info, up front

A mobile app that delivers accessibility information to people with special needs searching for accessible services in their local area. Users can contribute to the app and take advantage of accommodations by highlighting accessible services in public and commercial settings.

01 · Research

42.5 million Americans live with a disability

“Overall, there are about 42.5 million Americans with disabilities, making up 13% of the civilian noninstitutionalized population, according to U.S. Census Bureau data from 2021. This group includes people with hearing, vision, cognitive, walking, self-care or independent living difficulties.”Pew Research Center

People who need accessibility services often don’t know whether they’ll be welcome in their local area. Often it’s only after they’ve traveled to a destination that they find out it doesn’t have the accessibility services they need. That led us to research all the ways services fall short on sharing accessibility information. Before approaching the designs and the app’s content, we researched how available or scarce these services are on other platforms, and how to build inclusion in.
